This effectively turns your system tray menu into a Dashboard of Toggles. You can have "Frontend" and "Backend" both running simultaneously (perhaps a long build is running in the background while you code), and you must manually choose when to stop each one.
The "True Multitasking" Design
Here is how the UI and Logic adapt to this new requirement:
1. The Tray Menu (The "Toggle" List)
Instead of a single "Current Task," your menu becomes a list of active contexts.
    Top Section (Active Tasks):
        [ ■ ] Frontend Refactor (00:45) - RUNNING
        [ ■ ] Database Migration (00:10) - RUNNING
    Middle Section (Paused/Recent):
        [ ▶ ] Reply to Emails (01:20)
        [ ▶ ] Team Meeting
    Action:
        Clicking [ ■ ] Stops that specific timer.
        Clicking [ ▶ ] Starts that specific timer (without stopping others).
2. The Tray Icon
Since multiple things are happening, the icon text (if enabled) will likely summarize the state, e.g., (2 Active) or just show a colored dot.
3. The Visualization (Overlapping Gantt)
This makes the "Timeline View" even more critical. In the "End of Day" report, you will see overlapping bars.
    X-Axis: Time of day.
    Y-Axis: Tasks.
    Overlap: If you run Task A and B from 10:00 to 11:00, you will see two bars stacked vertically covering that hour.
    Math: Your "Total Task Hours" might be 10 hours, even if you only sat at the computer for 8 hours.
